# Persuasion architecture

How to structure an argument so a skeptical decision-maker says yes. Read this when outlining or when tailoring to an audience.

## The pyramid: answer first

Structure every deck as a pyramid (Minto). One governing thought at the top: the recommendation. Beneath it, 2 to 4 key arguments. Beneath each argument, the evidence. The deck presents the pyramid top-down: conclusion, then support.

Why: decision-makers process a conclusion-first argument as "verify or reject," which is fast and feels rigorous. They process an evidence-first argument as "where is this going?", which reads as either insecurity or a trap. Suspense works in film, not in budget meetings.

**Slide 2 formula (SCQA, compressed to four sentences):**

- **Situation:** one sentence of shared, undisputed context.
- **Complication:** what changed or what breaks if nothing is done.
- **Question:** implicit; the reader should already be asking it.
- **Answer:** the recommendation, the headline number, and the ask with its date.

If slide 2 leaked to the decision-maker with no other slides attached, it should still win on its own.

## Vertical and horizontal logic

- **Vertical (the title test):** action titles read in sequence must reconstruct the full argument. Test by listing only the titles. If a stranger can't reproduce the case from them, retitle before redesigning.
- **Horizontal:** each slide's body proves its own title and nothing else. Evidence that proves a different point belongs on that other slide or in the appendix. Body content that doesn't prove the title gets cut.

## Influence mechanics, and why each works

Use these deliberately. Each one maps to how people actually decide; none survives being faked.

**Anchoring.** The first number shown becomes the reference point for every number after it. Show the sized opportunity before the cost, and the partner's upside before your ask. A $400K ask lands differently after a $1.4B market slide than before it. Anchor honestly. An anchor the audience later discovers was inflated converts to permanent distrust.

**Reference class (social proof).** "Protocol A ran this structure and kept 63% of TVL after emissions ended" beats any projection, because it moves the plan from speculation to precedent. Decision-makers don't fund leaps; they fund the next member of a class that already works. Always normalize comparables (per-$ efficiency, retention) so they're evidence, not decoration.

**Loss framing, once.** People weigh losses roughly twice as heavily as gains. One slide (usually "Why now") should state what waiting costs: share consolidating elsewhere, a window with a date on it. Use it exactly once and ground it in fact. Repeated loss framing reads as fear-selling and collapses trust.

**Disclosure against interest.** Self-declared risks are the strongest credibility device available. The audience has a mental risk ledger; if the deck's ledger is more honest than theirs, they relax and lend the rest of the deck their trust. If the deck hides a risk they've already thought of, everything else becomes suspect. Corollary: kill criteria ("we stop at day 45 if TVL < $8M") show you've priced your own failure, which paradoxically makes people fund you.

**Commitment momentum.** A dated, small, concrete next step ("sign the MOU by 31 Jul", "30-minute working session Thursday") converts agreement into motion. Big vague asks stall because they can't be scheduled. Design the yes to be cheap and the momentum to compound (30/60/90).

**Effort justification, avoided.** Never make the audience work to extract the point. Every unit of effort spent decoding a slide is deducted from the credibility budget.

## Audience modes

The argument is the same; the ordering, lead metric, and tone change.

### Partner / BD mode

- **They optimize:** their fees, their users, their TVL, their roadmap cost.
- **Lead:** their upside, quantified, slides 1 to 4. Your benefit appears late and briefly: they know you benefit; belaboring it wastes their time.
- **Cost framing:** quantify their lift in eng-days and calendar time. "Two engineers, six days, we supply the contracts and the dashboard" beats "simple integration."
- **Proof:** what comparable partners gained, normalized.
- **Common failure:** pitching your strategy instead of their P&L. They do not care about your KPIs.

### Exec / internal mode

- **They optimize:** metric impact per dollar, risk to the existing book, opportunity cost.
- **Lead:** the metric moved and the cost of inaction, with the spend and payback beside it.
- **Structure the decision:** execs choose between options, not yes/no. Present A (recommended), B, C with one-line trade-offs. A lone option invites "come back with alternatives"; three options with a recommendation invites a decision.
- **Include:** kill criteria and a review date. Asking for reversible permission is far easier to grant than irreversible commitment.
- **Common failure:** burying the spend, or presenting one option as inevitable.

### Investor mode

- **They optimize:** return per unit of risk, downside protection, liquidity.
- **Lead:** the return math: entry, base-case multiple, and the bear case with its probability. Showing the downside first signals you've priced it, which is what they're checking.
- **Must survive:** "why now" and "why hasn't someone already done this?" If the deck has no honest answer, the opportunity as framed doesn't exist.
- **Common failure:** all-upside decks. Investors mentally halve every projection they see; a deck that pre-halves its own bear case gets taken at face value.

## Objection pre-emption method

1. List every objection this audience could raise. Aim for ten.
2. Sort by lethality: which one, unanswered, kills the deal?
3. The top 3 to 5 go on the risk slide: **risk → mitigation → residual** ("what's still true after mitigation"). Stating residual risk is what separates honesty from theater; "fully mitigated" is a tell that the analysis is shallow.
4. The rest go to the appendix as pre-answered Q&A with data.
5. The three hardest questions get a dedicated appendix slide each. In the meeting, "slide A3 covers that" is a power move that no improvised answer matches.

## Order of operations when writing

1. The ask, in one sentence, with its date.
2. The three arguments that justify it (these become sections).
3. Action titles for all ten slides; run the title test.
4. Evidence per slide: one idea, one proof.
5. Only then: design.
